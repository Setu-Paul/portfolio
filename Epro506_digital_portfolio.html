A professional digital portfolio for early childhood educators working with children, families and communities in contemporary Australian society. Five evidence-based contexts — grounded in the EYLF V2.0, NQS, and current Australian policy and research.

Setu Paul  |  Student ID: IkO3012951  |  EPRO506 · IKON Institute of Australia  |  18 August 2026

Early childhood services are not separate from the society in which children grow. They are community institutions shaped by economic conditions, family relationships, cultural contexts, health systems, public policy and emergencies. Bronfenbrenner (1979) places the child within interacting systems — from close relationships to government policy and social values. Resilience, as Ungar (2011) argues, develops when children access safe relationships, cultural continuity, material resources and responsive institutions. Each section of this portfolio analyses one contemporary context through that dual lens: structural challenge and family strength.

Understanding the Context

Bronfenbrenner (1979) Ecological SystemsBourdieu (1986) Forms of CapitalConger et al. (2010) Family Stress ModelMasten (2014) Resilience

Economic hardship occurs when a family's income and material resources are insufficient to meet essential needs — housing, food, transport, healthcare and education. In Australia, the Australian Council of Social Service and UNSW Sydney (2023) report approximately 761,000 children live below the poverty line, roughly one in eight. In Queensland, the Child Poverty Alliance (2023) estimates that over 180,000 children live in households experiencing financial stress, with Sunshine Coast and Moreton Bay regions among those with growing proportions of low-income renters displaced by post-pandemic housing market pressures. These figures reflect structural conditions — wage stagnation, social housing shortfall, casualised employment, disability and discrimination — not personal failure.

Bourdieu's (1986) concept of capital illuminates how poverty restricts not only financial resources but social networks (social capital) and institutionally valued knowledge (cultural capital), compounding disadvantage across generations. The family stress model (Conger et al., 2010) explains how financial pressure can erode parental wellbeing and disrupt consistent caregiving — though protective factors, including access to quality early childhood education, can interrupt this pathway. Bronfenbrenner's (1979) ecological systems theory situates the child within interacting environments: federal welfare settings, Queensland housing markets and the family home all shape the material conditions of daily life. Resilience research (Masten, 2014) confirms that stable relationships, predictable routines and responsive services can moderate developmental risks even under conditions of significant economic adversity.

Impact on Children, Families and Services

Financial hardship restricts access to nutritious food, safe play environments, consistent healthcare and stable housing — each with documented implications for cognitive development, language acquisition, physical health and school readiness (AIHW, 2023). Children experiencing housing instability in Queensland — including those in temporary caravan parks, couch-surfing arrangements or overcrowded dwellings — may attend early childhood services inconsistently, present with heightened anxiety or struggle to form stable peer relationships. These presentations require compassionate, trauma-informed interpretation, not deficit labelling.

Families may experience shame when asked to supply materials, pay excursion fees or explain absences. Competitive fundraising, food drive activities and visible "needy family" supports can intensify exclusion and stigma. A more equitable service design universalises support — breakfast programs, borrowing libraries, spare clothing, no-cost excursions — so that no child is identified by what they receive. Services must also recognise that cost barriers, transport limitations and stigma can prevent the most disadvantaged families from accessing the programs designed to support them, creating an inverse equity problem that requires proactive, structural response.

Social Policy and Australian Responses

Australian Early Years Strategy 2024–2034 (Department of Education, 2024)
A whole-of-government framework for children to thrive, emphasising integrated, child-centred policy design and early intervention across sectors.
Provides the first nationally coordinated framework linking early childhood with housing, health and income support policy.
As a framework document, its impact depends on state implementation. Queensland's response through the Starting and Growing Strong Strategy 2017–2027 varies in reach across regional and remote areas.
Child Care Subsidy — Services Australia
Reduces financial barriers to early childhood education and care. Activity-based eligibility allows most working families to access subsidised care.
Has expanded participation for many Queensland families, particularly in dual-income households in metropolitan and regional areas.
Activity-based requirements may exclude families without stable employment — including those experiencing family violence, disability or casualised work. The most disadvantaged families may access the least subsidised care (ACOSS & UNSW, 2023).
Queensland Social Housing: Homes for Queenslanders Strategy 2024
Commits to increasing social and affordable housing supply across Queensland, acknowledging housing as a social determinant of children's outcomes.
Acknowledges housing affordability as an urgent child wellbeing issue and commits specific targets for Queensland construction.
Queensland's social housing waitlist exceeds 48,000 households (AIHW, 2023). The strategy's supply targets will not resolve current unmet need within the timeframes relevant to young children in the system today.

Five Evidence-Based Strategies for Practice

1
Non-stigmatising, relational engagement

Raise practical concerns privately. Use respectful, non-judgemental language and obtain informed consent before referrals. Treat families as decision-makers in their children's education and care, not passive recipients of professional assessment.

2
Universal material support

Design support so all children benefit without identification: breakfast available to all, spare clothing in the bathroom, sunscreen provided, excursions no-cost. Remove the moment where a disadvantaged child is distinguished from their peers.

3
Predictable routines and educator continuity

Assign consistent key educators. Use visual timetables and transition objects. When a family moves — increasingly common in Queensland's rental market — proactively support records transfer and connection with the receiving service.

4
Flexible access and communication

Offer varied communication channels, translated information, flexible fee arrangements and the option of remote participation in meetings. Reduce the administrative burden that falls disproportionately on families managing multiple stressors simultaneously.

5
Warm referral with systemic advocacy

With consent, contact services alongside families — not instead of them. Assist with forms. Document systemic barriers and report these through appropriate channels. Advocacy within professional scope is not optional: it is part of the EYLF's commitment to inclusion.

Understanding the Context

Bowlby (1988) Attachment TheoryBronfenbrenner (1979) Ecological SystemsPutnam (2000) Social CapitalMasten (2014) Resilience

Out-of-home care (OOHC) refers to formal arrangements — foster care, kinship care or residential care — when children cannot safely remain in their family home. The AIHW (2023) reports approximately 46,000 Australian children in OOHC on any given night in 2021–22, at a rate of 8.2 per 1,000. In Queensland, the Department of Children, Youth Justice and Multicultural Affairs (DCYJMA, 2023) reports that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children represent 44% of children in Queensland OOHC despite comprising approximately 10% of the Queensland child population — a figure that demands structural, not individual, explanation rooted in the ongoing effects of colonisation, systemic racism, poverty and family service underfunding.

Bowlby's (1988) attachment theory is foundational: children who have experienced abuse, neglect or unpredictable caregiving may develop disrupted attachment patterns affecting trust, emotional regulation and the capacity to form and sustain relationships. These are adaptive responses to unsafe environments, not pathological deficits. Bronfenbrenner's (1979) ecological framework illuminates how children entering OOHC simultaneously lose primary caregivers, siblings, schools, communities and familiar daily routines — a magnitude of system disruption that has no parallel in typical development. Resilience theory (Masten, 2014) offers the critical counterpoint: children's capacity for recovery is not fixed. It emerges from access to consistent, warm relationships, stable environments and responsive institutions. A quality early childhood service can be among the most significant protective environments a child in care accesses.

Impact on Children, Families and Services

Children in OOHC are at elevated population-level risk for developmental, social-emotional and mental health challenges, though individual outcomes vary considerably and many children in care demonstrate significant strengths and resilience (AIHW, 2023). Pre-placement trauma, not placement itself, is typically the primary driver of vulnerability. Children may present in early childhood settings with hypervigilance, difficulty managing transitions, challenges forming trusting relationships with educators, delayed language development or emotional dysregulation. These presentations require compassionate, curious interpretation — they are communications of unmet need in environments that have not previously been safe.

For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children in OOHC, identity and cultural connection are not supplementary concerns — they are foundational rights and critical protective factors (SNAICC, 2020). Early childhood services must actively support cultural connection, not merely tolerate it. Multiple placement changes — experienced by a significant proportion of Queensland children in OOHC — compound educational disruption, break trusted relationships and undermine the developmental continuity that secure attachment requires. Services must prepare clear protocols for collection arrangements, communication with carers and caseworkers, and mandatory reporting obligations — and must train all staff to understand these as standard professional practice, not exceptional circumstances.

Social Policy and Australian Responses

Safe and Supported: National Framework for Protecting Australia's Children 2021–2031 (DSS, 2021)
Provides national direction for prevention, early intervention, family preservation and support for children in care to thrive. Explicitly addresses overrepresentation of First Nations children as a national priority.
Provides a coordinated, prevention-first framework that recognises the long-term costs of the child protection system and the value of early support.
Numbers in OOHC nationally have not significantly declined under previous frameworks. Prevention investment remains insufficient relative to crisis-response expenditure. Queensland's implementation through the Child Protection and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2023 is still embedding.
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Child Placement Principle (ATSI CPP)
Requires that Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children be placed, in order of priority, with kin, then community, then non-Indigenous carers who can actively support cultural connection. Has legislative standing in Queensland under the Child Protection Act 1999.
Represents a legislated commitment to cultural connection as a child safety and wellbeing matter, not an optional preference.
The ATSI CPP is inconsistently implemented in Queensland. Many Aboriginal children remain in non-Indigenous placements without adequate cultural support (SNAICC, 2020). Legislative requirement and operational practice continue to diverge.
Queensland Child Protection Act 1999 — Mandatory Reporting
Requires all early childhood educators, among others, to report reasonable suspicion of harm or risk of harm to a child to the Child Safety intake line (1800 177 135) without delay.
Creates a clear, legally enforceable obligation that protects children and clarifies the educator's role.
Mandatory reporting training varies significantly in quality across Queensland services. Some educators remain uncertain about thresholds for reporting, creating inconsistency in child protection practice.

Five Evidence-Based Strategies for Practice

1
Key educator continuity and relationship-based pedagogy

Assign a consistent, primary educator as the relational anchor for each child in OOHC. Gradually introduce a second trusted educator for continuity. Create predictable daily rituals — consistent greetings, farewell routines, comfort check-ins — that build the predictability attachment security requires.

2
Detailed, regularly reviewed settling and transition plan

Record comfort objects, words used for family members, collection arrangements, sensory preferences, what helps after contact visits, and what the child needs when transitioning between environments. Review at every placement change and share with receiving services.

3
Neutral, inclusive family language throughout the curriculum

Use 'families', 'grown-ups' and the child's own terms. Display diverse family configurations without presenting any one form as normative. Avoid activities — family tree projects, mother's day cards — that assume a particular family structure.

4
Collaborative documentation with carers and caseworkers

With legal clarity and informed consent, share factual, documented observations with carers and allocated Queensland Child Safety workers. Never carry messages between adults in the child's network, and never ask children to explain their living situation.

5
Culturally safe practice for First Nations children in care

For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children in OOHC, actively pursue connection to local Community Controlled Organisations, Elders and cultural activities. This is not optional — it is a rights-based obligation under the ATSI CPP and a critical protective factor for the child's identity and wellbeing.

Understanding the Context

Bronfenbrenner (1979) Chronosystem — colonial historyMoll et al. (1992) Funds of KnowledgeCrenshaw (1989) IntersectionalityMasten (2014) Resilience — cultural continuity as protective

Cultural diversity in Australia is both ancient and contemporary. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples are the custodians of the world's oldest living continuous cultures, with connections to Country spanning tens of thousands of years across more than 250 distinct language groups. The IKON Institute of Australia sits on the lands of the Kabi Kabi (also written Gubbi Gubbi) peoples, and services in this region have a particular responsibility to engage authentically with Kabi Kabi community organisations, Elders and knowledge holders. The 2021 Census records that 27.6% of Australians were born overseas and 22.8% spoke a language other than English at home (ABS, 2022), reflecting the breadth of cultural diversity that early childhood services are expected to welcome and affirm.

Funds of knowledge theory (Moll et al., 1992) asks educators to recognise the rich, historically developed knowledge embedded in household and community practices — knowledge that is often invisible or undervalued within mainstream educational settings. Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989) explains how experiences are shaped by overlapping social positions — race, gender, disability, class, religion and visa status — and cautions against single-axis analysis. For First Nations families, culturally safe practice must acknowledge sovereignty, ongoing connection to Country, the profound and continuing harms of colonisation and community-controlled organisations' right to determine what is appropriate for their children and families. Cultural responsiveness is not achieved through NAIDOC Week activities or multicultural dress-up days: it requires examining power, forming genuine and sustained community partnerships and changing institutional systems, not asking families to adapt to institutions (ACECQA, 2024).

Impact on Children, Families and Services

When language, names, family structures and cultural knowledge are genuinely reflected in an early childhood environment, children experience belonging, identity affirmation and engagement with learning. When they encounter racism — including the everyday racism of mispronunciation, tokenism, invisible curricula and deficit assumptions — they may withdraw, suppress parts of their identity or carry the psychological weight of being treated as representative of an entire culture. For refugee-background and recently arrived children, unfamiliar enrolment systems, limited professional interpreter access, transport barriers and distrust arising from prior experiences with authorities and institutions can restrict family engagement with services.

Services must guard actively against within-group stereotyping: there is no single Aboriginal, Muslim, African, Asian or refugee perspective. Cultural safety requires governance-level institutional change — paid cultural expertise, accredited interpreter use, anti-racism policies and procedures, correct name pronunciation, bilingual learning environments and genuine sustained consultation with local communities — not supplementary activities bolted onto an unchanged mainstream program. Educators must distinguish cultural difference from developmental concern and must never use children as interpreters in significant conversations with adults. Within the Sunshine Coast region specifically, services should engage directly with the Kabi Kabi Gangalidda Aboriginal Corporation and local ACCOs to inform and co-design culturally appropriate programs.

Social Policy and Australian Responses

National Agreement on Closing the Gap (Coalition of Peaks & Australian Government, 2020)
Developed in genuine partnership between the Coalition of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peak Organisations and all Australian governments. Target 4 aims for 55% of First Nations children developmentally on track at school entry by 2031.
First major national policy framework developed through genuine co-design with First Nations peoples, representing a structural shift from government-determined to community-determined targets.
Progress on most Closing the Gap targets nationally remains insufficient or is worsening. Structural and community-controlled approaches are required. Deficit explanations for ongoing gaps must be actively rejected in favour of systemic analysis.
EYLF V2.0 — Cultural Responsiveness as Foundational Practice (DoE, 2022)
Embeds Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives and cultural responsiveness as core expectations of all approved early childhood services — not optional or additional themes.
Positions cultural responsiveness as a professional obligation for all Australian educators, regardless of the cultural composition of their current enrolment.
Implementation varies significantly. The Framework's intent requires translation into genuine local community relationships that cannot be replaced by commercially produced 'culturally inclusive' resources or curriculum packs.
Queensland Multicultural Action Plan 2022–2024 and Settlement Services
Queensland Government commitment to welcoming and integrating newly arrived families through coordinated settlement services, community organisations and English language support.
Provides a state-level policy framework that supports schools, services and community organisations in responding to cultural and linguistic diversity.
Short-term settlement funding cycles limit relationship-building and service continuity. Services in high-settlement areas like Sunshine Coast require sustained, not time-limited, partnership investment.

Five Evidence-Based Strategies for Practice

1
Co-design curriculum and governance with Kabi Kabi community

Form ongoing, paid relationships with local Kabi Kabi Elders, ACCOs and cultural leaders. Act on the advice received. Do not treat consultation as a one-off audit or a formality before implementing a pre-determined program. Relationships take time and require demonstrated reciprocity.

2
Sustain and celebrate home languages and cultural identities

Learn key words in families' home languages. Use bilingual picture books, audio and visual materials. Invite family stories, songs and recipes into the curriculum. Reassure families explicitly that home language supports, not hinders, English acquisition and cognitive development.

3
Embed anti-racism as everyday institutional practice

Interrupt stereotyping and discriminatory comments immediately. Document incidents. Review recruitment, enrolment, curriculum and governance processes for embedded bias. Teach children to notice, name and respond to unfair exclusion — not as a special lesson, but as part of everyday community and social learning.

4
Practise cultural humility, not cultural expertise

Ask rather than assume. Pronounce names correctly and without commentary. Reflect on your own cultural positioning and power. Accept correction without defensiveness. Never ask one family member or community person to represent their entire culture or group.

5
Make systems genuinely accessible across language and culture

Use accredited professional interpreters — not children, siblings or community volunteers — for important conversations. Provide visual enrolment information. Offer flexible meeting times and locations. Proactively check whether visa status, literacy levels or unfamiliarity with Australian systems creates access barriers for specific families.

Understanding the Context

Bowlby (1988) Attachment TheorySocial Determinants of HealthMasten (2014) ResilienceSAMHSA (2014) Trauma-Informed Practice

Health and wellbeing contexts in early childhood practice include parental mental illness, perinatal anxiety and depression, substance use, grief, disability and their intersections with poverty, housing insecurity and social isolation. The AIHW (2022) reports approximately one in five Australians experiences a mental health condition annually — a figure that represents not pathology but the human response to lives that are sometimes overwhelmed by structural and relational challenges. PANDA (2023) estimates one in five new mothers and one in ten new fathers experience perinatal anxiety or depression. The vast majority of parents who experience mental illness provide loving, safe, committed care for their children. This is not an exceptional finding requiring qualification — it is the baseline from which all practice in this context should begin.

Bowlby's (1988) attachment theory provides critical analytical grounding: parental mental illness, when severe or unsupported, may affect the consistency and sensitivity of caregiving at critical developmental periods. However, this is not a deterministic or universal pathway — it is a population-level association mediated by a range of protective factors including treatment access, social support, stable housing and quality early childhood education (Masten, 2014). Social determinants of health situate parental mental illness within its structural context: poverty, housing insecurity, domestic violence, discrimination and limited healthcare access all elevate mental illness rates in ways that individual therapeutic interventions alone cannot resolve. Trauma-informed practice (SAMHSA, 2014) understands behaviour as an adaptive response to overwhelming experience and prioritises safety, trust, choice, collaboration and empowerment in all interactions with children and families.

Impact on Children, Families and Services

Children whose parents experience severe or untreated mental illness may face elevated population-level risk of inconsistent caregiving, disrupted routines and emotional unavailability (AIHW, 2022). Some children take on caring roles for parents experiencing significant illness, carrying responsibilities well beyond their developmental capacity. In early childhood settings, children may present with anxiety, difficulty managing transitions, emotional dysregulation or challenges forming trusting relationships — all of which require compassionate, curious interpretation rather than diagnostic labelling or punitive responses.

For families, parental mental illness can create barriers to service engagement: stigma, reduced energy during difficult periods, fear of judgement or child removal, and the practical management challenges of navigating a health condition while parenting young children. Services that respond only in crisis miss substantial prevention opportunities. Everyday early childhood practice can actively support family mental health through the quality of relational environments offered, emotional literacy embedded throughout the curriculum, sensory regulation opportunities, outdoor play, flexible communication and genuine family partnership. Educators themselves require supervision, role clarity, access to debriefing and realistic workload expectations — secondary traumatic stress is a documented risk in services supporting families with high adversity, and it is a system responsibility, not an individual failing.

Five Evidence-Based Strategies for Practice

1
Create relational safety through environmental and interpersonal predictability

Use calm, consistent educator voices, predictable daily sequences, advance notice of changes and a warm settled physical environment. Repair relationship ruptures when they occur — the repair itself is a powerful modelling of how trusted adults respond when things go wrong.

2
Teach co-regulation before expecting self-regulation

Offer proximity, rhythmic movement, breathing, sensory tools and calm quiet spaces. Do not demand verbal reflection or emotional naming while a child is dysregulated. The adult's regulated nervous system is the intervention — be present before expecting the child to process.

3
Use strengths-based family conversations with genuine curiosity

Describe observations in specific, non-diagnostic language. Ask what works at home and what adults in the child's network notice. Identify the child's existing protective relationships. Agree on one or two achievable shared goals — not a comprehensive intervention plan.

4
Follow child safety and mandatory reporting procedures without delay

Document exact observations and verbatim disclosures. Avoid leading questions. Consult the nominated supervisor. Meet Queensland's mandatory reporting obligation to Child Safety (1800 177 135) when there are reasonable grounds for concern — do not delay to gather more information.

5
Build a supported, supervised and professionally boundaried workforce

Provide regular reflective supervision, clear role boundaries, access to structured debriefing and employee assistance programs. Staff supporting families with complex mental health presentations need system-level containment — not individual resilience as a substitute for adequate professional support.

Understanding the Context

Bronfenbrenner (1979) Ecological SystemsCrenshaw (1989) IntersectionalitySAMHSA (2014) Trauma-Informed PracticeMasten (2014) Resilience — protective factors under chronic stress

Domestic and family violence (DFV) encompasses any pattern of behaviour in an intimate or family relationship used to coerce, control or harm — including physical violence, sexual assault, emotional and psychological abuse, financial abuse and coercive control. Coercive control is not an escalation of conflict: it is a sustained pattern of surveillance, isolation, humiliation and domination that can be more harmful to children and non-offending partners than physical violence alone. The ABS Personal Safety Survey (2023) reports that approximately one in six Australian women and one in seventeen men have experienced physical and/or sexual violence from a current or former partner since age 15. DFV is the leading cause of homelessness for women and children in Australia and a significant driver of child protection notifications.

In Queensland, the Domestic and Family Violence Protection Act 2012 defines DFV broadly and specifically includes coercive control as a form of domestic violence — creating legal context that educators must understand. Bronfenbrenner's (1979) ecological systems theory frames DFV as both a microsystem experience (within the household) and a macrosystem problem rooted in gender inequality, economic power imbalances and social norms that tolerate male control over female partners and children. Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989) illuminates how overlapping dimensions of identity — Indigenous status, disability, visa status, sexuality, geographic isolation — compound vulnerability and constrain help-seeking. A strengths-based approach recognises the extraordinary courage, protective capacity and resilience demonstrated by people who have experienced DFV: non-offending parents typically go to great lengths to protect their children, often at significant personal cost and risk.

Impact on Children, Families and Services

Children who have witnessed or experienced DFV are at elevated population-level risk for developmental, social-emotional and mental health challenges, though individual outcomes depend significantly on protective factors and available support (AIHW, 2022). Chronic stress in environments characterised by fear, unpredictability and adult distress affects children's neurological development — particularly the stress-response systems and executive functioning capacities that underpin learning and self-regulation. In Queensland early childhood settings, children from DFV-affected families may present with hypervigilance, sleep disturbances, regression, aggressive play, unusual fearfulness or emotional volatility. These are adaptive responses to unsafe environments — not conduct requiring management — and they demand compassionate, curious professional interpretation.

For non-offending parents, the impact of DFV on parenting reflects the effects of sustained trauma and coercive control — not individual weakness or poor decision-making. Services must maintain non-judgemental, compassionate relationships with non-offending parents while recognising that their safety and the child's safety are interconnected. Families escaping DFV in the Sunshine Coast and broader Queensland region may face emergency relocation, rapid service transitions, loss of housing, documents and social networks. Services must protect confidentiality absolutely, facilitate continuity of care across service boundaries and refuse to disclose family location or contact information under any circumstances — including to the person using violence.

Social Policy and Australian Responses

National Plan to End Violence against Women and Children 2022–2032 (DSS, 2022)
Australia's overarching framework for preventing and responding to DFV and sexual violence, structured across prevention, early intervention, response and recovery. Explicitly addresses primary prevention through gender inequality as the root cause.
Positions DFV as a social and structural problem requiring whole-community prevention, not only crisis services. Includes specific attention to children as victim-survivors in their own right.
Wait times for crisis accommodation in Queensland remain lengthy, particularly outside major cities. Culturally appropriate services for First Nations women and CALD communities remain insufficient despite explicit policy commitments.
Queensland Domestic and Family Violence Protection Act 2012 (amended 2023)
Provides Queensland's legal framework for DFV protection orders, safety planning and service response. The 2023 amendments introduce coercive control as a criminal offence and expand protections.
Queensland is among the first Australian jurisdictions to criminalise coercive control — a significant recognition of the broader patterns of DFV beyond physical violence.
Criminalising coercive control creates new responsibilities for service systems including early childhood. Educators need training on recognising patterns of coercive control — not only physical indicators — to apply mandatory reporting obligations appropriately.
Queensland Mandatory Reporting — Child Protection Act 1999
Requires all early childhood educators to report reasonable suspicion of harm or risk of harm to a child — including exposure to domestic violence — to the Child Safety intake line (1800 177 135) without delay.
Creates a clear, enforceable legal obligation that protects children whose parents may not be able to self-report due to safety risks.
Many educators remain uncertain about the threshold for reporting children's exposure to DFV, which may result in underreporting. Regular mandatory reporting training specific to DFV contexts is essential in all Queensland early childhood services.

Five Evidence-Based Strategies for Practice

1
Trauma-informed environmental design and relational safety

Maintain calm, consistent, predictable environments. Use low-sensory transitions, consistent educator voices and physical spaces that do not feel threatening or chaotic. Interpret hypervigilance, aggression and regression as responses to prior threat — not as conduct requiring correction.

2
Age-appropriate body safety education within a comprehensive child rights curriculum

Incorporate body safety education as part of everyday curriculum — not as a reactive response to identified concerns. Use Australian-developed resources (Sanders, 2011, 2015). Teach correct anatomical language, safe and unsafe touch, and that worry secrets should always be shared with a trusted adult. Follow up all disclosures promptly and without leading questions.

3
Non-judgemental, confidential practice with non-offending parents

Listen. Affirm experience. Share information about available services without pressure, unsolicited advice or commentary on relationship decisions. Never disclose a family's location, contact details or service attendance to any third party — including a partner, ex-partner or family member — without explicit written consent from the family.

4
Support continuity of care during crisis and relocation

Maintain flexible attendance records for families experiencing DFV. When families need to relocate quickly, facilitate rapid documentation transfer, provide a warm written handover to the receiving service and confirm the child's details are updated and confidential at the new service. Contact the family (via a safe number) to confirm successful transition.

5
Fulfil mandatory reporting obligations promptly, accurately and without delay

Document exact observations, specific behaviours and verbatim disclosures in factual, non-interpretive language. Consult the nominated supervisor and Queensland's Child Safety intake (1800 177 135) when there are reasonable grounds for concern. Do not wait for certainty — mandatory reporting is triggered by reasonable suspicion, not proof.

In early childhood settings, it is also one of the most sensitive, complex and legally significant contexts educators navigate.
