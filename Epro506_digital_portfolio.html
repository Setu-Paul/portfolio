Context 01 — Economic

Child Poverty and Financial Hardship

Structural inequality, housing stress and the equity responsibilities of early childhood services — with specific reference to Queensland families and local Sunshine Coast service responses.

1 in 8Australian children below poverty line (ACOSS & UNSW, 2023)
761,000children in poverty nationally — structural, not individual
42%of lone-parent families cannot raise $2,000 for urgent need (ABS, 2026)
NQS QA6Collaborative partnerships — equity-based referral framework
Understanding the Context
Bronfenbrenner (1979) Ecological SystemsBourdieu (1986) Forms of CapitalConger et al. (2010) Family Stress ModelMasten (2014) Resilience Theory

Economic hardship occurs when a family's income and material resources are insufficient to meet essential needs including housing, food, transport, healthcare and education. The Australian Council of Social Service and UNSW Sydney (2023) report approximately 761,000 children below the poverty line — about one in eight. In Queensland, the Sunshine Coast and Moreton Bay regions have experienced significant housing pressure following post-pandemic population growth, displacing low-income renters from affordable early childhood services. These figures reflect structural conditions — wage stagnation, social housing shortfall and casualised employment — not personal failure.

Bourdieu's (1986) concept of capital explains how poverty restricts not only financial resources but social networks and institutionally valued knowledge. The family stress model (Conger et al., 2010) demonstrates how financial pressure can erode parental wellbeing and disrupt caregiving. Resilience research (Masten, 2014) confirms that stable relationships, predictable routines and responsive services moderate developmental risk even under sustained economic adversity.

Impact on Children, Families and Services

Financial hardship restricts access to nutritious food, safe play environments and consistent healthcare. Children experiencing housing instability may attend services inconsistently and present with heightened anxiety. Services must protect dignity through universal rather than targeted supports — breakfast for all, spare clothing accessible to all, no-cost excursions — so no child is identified by what they receive.

Social Policy and Australian Responses
Australian Early Years Strategy 2024–2034 (Department of Education, 2024)

A whole-of-government framework linking early childhood with housing, health and income support — the first nationally coordinated approach of its kind.

StrengthProvides a coherent national framework treating early childhood as a cross-sectoral responsibility.
Critical NoteImpact depends on Queensland implementation. Regional variation limits equitable reach in outer Sunshine Coast growth corridors.
Child Care Subsidy — Services Australia

Reduces financial barriers to early childhood education. Activity-based eligibility allows most working families to access subsidised care.

StrengthHas expanded participation for many Queensland families, particularly dual-income households.
Critical NoteActivity-based requirements may exclude families without stable employment. The most disadvantaged families may access the least subsidised care (ACOSS & UNSW, 2023).
Five Evidence-Based Strategies for Practice
01
Non-Stigmatising Relational Engagement

Raise practical concerns privately with respectful, non-judgemental language. Obtain informed consent before referrals. Treat families as decision-makers, not passive recipients.

02
Universal Material Support

Design supports so all children benefit without identification. Breakfast for all, spare clothing accessible to all, excursions free of cost.

03
Predictable Routines and Educator Continuity

Assign consistent key educators. Use visual timetables and transition objects. Proactively support records transfer when families relocate.

04
Flexible Access and Communication

Offer varied communication channels, translated information and flexible fee arrangements. Reduce administrative burden on families managing multiple stressors.

05
Warm Referral with Systemic Advocacy

With consent, contact services alongside families. Document systemic barriers and report them. Advocacy is part of the EYLF commitment to inclusion.

Context 02 — Social

Children in Out-of-Home Care

Attachment, placement stability, the Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Child Placement Principle, and the early childhood service as a protective environment in Queensland.

46,000children in OOHC nationally on any given night (AIHW, 2023)
10×rate for First Nations children vs non-Indigenous nationally
44%of Queensland OOHC children are Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander
NQS QA2Children's health, safety and wellbeing — the core obligation
Understanding the Context
Bowlby (1988) Attachment TheoryBronfenbrenner (1979) Ecological SystemsPutnam (2000) Social CapitalMasten (2014) Resilience

Out-of-home care (OOHC) refers to formal arrangements — foster care, kinship care or residential care — when children cannot safely remain in their family home. The AIHW (2023) reports approximately 46,000 Australian children in OOHC in 2021–22. In Queensland, the Department of Children, Youth Justice and Multicultural Affairs (DCYJMA, 2023) reports Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children represent 44% of Queensland OOHC despite comprising approximately 10% of the child population — requiring structural, not individual, explanation.

Bowlby's (1988) attachment theory is foundational: children who have experienced abuse, neglect or unpredictable caregiving may develop disrupted attachment patterns. These are adaptive responses, not deficits. Resilience theory (Masten, 2014) confirms that recovery capacity emerges from consistent, warm relationships, stable environments and responsive institutions.

Two Family Circumstances — Social Context
1
Foster Care Families
Children placed in OOHC with non-related foster families experience the most significant relational disruption — separation from siblings, familiar routines, neighbourhood and school. Foster carers, though trained and well-intentioned, are strangers to the child. Children may present with hypervigilance, testing behaviour and resistance to forming new attachments, which are neurologically adaptive responses to prior unpredictability (Bowlby, 1988).

For early childhood services, children from foster families may arrive mid-year following placement decisions, with incomplete medical and developmental histories. Enrolment processes must be flexible, sensitive and coordinated with Queensland Child Safety workers.
Service Implication Assign a consistent key educator immediately. Prepare a detailed settling plan including comfort objects, communication preferences, collection arrangements and what helps after contact visits. Share only with authorised individuals.
2
Kinship Care Families
Kinship care — placement with grandparents, aunts, uncles or other family members — preserves cultural connection and relational familiarity but introduces unique stressors. Kinship carers are often older, experiencing their own health challenges, financially disadvantaged, and may not have anticipated taking on a primary caregiving role. They may also carry grief about the circumstances that required the placement.

For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children, kinship care is the preferred option under the ATSI Child Placement Principle (Child Protection Act 1999, Qld), as it maintains cultural continuity. However, many kinship carers lack access to carer support services and feel excluded from mainstream ECEC systems.
Service Implication Use inclusive, flexible family language. Acknowledge that kinship carers are the child's primary family for all purposes. Connect carers with Queensland Child Safety support services and local community resources.
Impact on Children, Families and Services

Children in OOHC face elevated population-level risk for developmental and social-emotional challenges, though outcomes vary and many demonstrate significant strengths (AIHW, 2023). For First Nations children, cultural connection is a foundational right and critical protective factor (SNAICC, 2020). Multiple placement changes compound educational disruption and break trusted relationships.

Social Policy and Australian Responses
Safe and Supported: National Framework 2021–2031 (DSS, 2021)

National direction for prevention, early intervention and support for children in care. Addresses First Nations overrepresentation as a national priority requiring structural reform.

StrengthPrevention-first framework acknowledging the long-term cost of crisis-only investment.
Critical NoteNumbers in OOHC have not declined under previous frameworks. Queensland implementation is still embedding.
ATSI Child Placement Principle (Child Protection Act 1999, Qld)

Requires placement priority for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children with kin, then community, then non-Indigenous carers who support cultural connection.

StrengthA legislated commitment to cultural connection as a child safety matter.
Critical NoteInconsistently implemented in Queensland. Many Aboriginal children remain in non-Indigenous placements without adequate cultural support (SNAICC, 2020).
Five Evidence-Based Strategies for Practice
01
Key Educator Continuity

Assign a consistent primary educator as the relational anchor. Create predictable daily rituals — consistent greetings, farewell routines, comfort check-ins.

02
Detailed Settling and Transition Plan

Record comfort objects, family language, collection arrangements and what helps after contact visits. Review at every placement change.

03
Neutral, Inclusive Family Language

Use 'families', 'grown-ups' and the child's own terms. Avoid activities such as Mother's Day cards that assume a particular family structure.

04
Collaborative Documentation

Share factual documented observations with carers and Queensland Child Safety workers with legal clarity and informed consent.

05
Culturally Safe Practice for First Nations Children

Actively pursue connection to local ACCOs, Elders and cultural activities for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children in OOHC.

Context 03 — Cultural & Diversity

First Nations and Culturally Diverse Families

Sovereignty, cultural safety, genuine partnership with Kabi Kabi community and responding to Australia's diverse families — with critical analysis of tokenism, institutional power and authentic inclusion.

27.6%of Australians born overseas — 2021 Census (ABS, 2022)
250+distinct First Nations language groups across Australia
22.8%spoke a language other than English at home — 2021 Census
Kabi KabiTraditional custodians of the Sunshine Coast and surrounding Country
Understanding the Context
Bronfenbrenner (1979) Chronosystem — colonial historyMoll et al. (1992) Funds of KnowledgeCrenshaw (1989) IntersectionalityMasten (2014) Resilience — cultural continuity as protective

Cultural diversity in Australia is both ancient and contemporary. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples are custodians of the world's oldest living continuous cultures, with connections to Country spanning tens of thousands of years. The Ikon Institute sits on the lands of the Kabi Kabi peoples. The 2021 Census records 27.6% of Australians born overseas and 22.8% speaking a language other than English at home (ABS, 2022).

Funds of knowledge theory (Moll et al., 1992) asks educators to recognise the rich, historically developed knowledge embedded in household and community practices. Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989) explains how overlapping dimensions — race, gender, disability, class, religion and visa status — compound vulnerability. Cultural responsiveness requires examining power, forming genuine community partnerships and changing institutional systems — it cannot be achieved through purchased cultural resource packs or NAIDOC Week activities alone (ACECQA, 2024).

Impact on Children, Families and Services

When language, names, family structures and cultural knowledge are genuinely reflected in the environment, children experience belonging, identity affirmation and engagement. When they encounter racism — including everyday racism of mispronunciation, tokenism and deficit assumptions — they may withdraw or suppress cultural identity. Services must guard against within-group stereotyping: there is no single Aboriginal, Muslim, African, Asian or refugee perspective.

Social Policy and Australian Responses
National Agreement on Closing the Gap (Coalition of Peaks & Australian Government, 2020)

Developed in partnership with the Coalition of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peak Organisations. Target 4 aims for 55% of First Nations children developmentally on track at school entry by 2031.

StrengthFirst nationally coordinated framework developed through genuine First Nations co-design.
Critical NoteProgress on most targets remains insufficient. Community-controlled approaches are essential. Deficit explanations must be actively rejected in favour of systemic and historical analysis.
EYLF V2.0 — Cultural Responsiveness (DoE, 2022)

Embeds Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander perspectives and cultural responsiveness as core expectations of all approved services.

StrengthPositions cultural responsiveness as a universal professional obligation.
Critical NoteImplementation varies significantly. Intent requires genuine local community relationships that cannot be replaced by commercially produced resources.
Five Evidence-Based Strategies for Practice
01
Co-Design with Kabi Kabi Community

Form ongoing, paid relationships with Kabi Kabi Elders and ACCOs. Act on advice received. Do not treat consultation as a formality before implementing a pre-determined program.

02
Sustain and Celebrate Home Languages

Learn key words in families' home languages. Use bilingual materials. Reassure families explicitly that home language supports English acquisition.

03
Embed Anti-Racism as Everyday Practice

Interrupt stereotyping immediately. Document incidents. Review recruitment, enrolment, curriculum and governance processes for embedded bias.

04
Practise Cultural Humility

Ask rather than assume. Pronounce names correctly. Accept correction without defensiveness. Never ask one person to represent their entire culture.

05
Genuinely Accessible Systems

Use accredited professional interpreters — not children or community volunteers — for important conversations. Provide visual enrolment information.

Context 04 — Health & Wellbeing

Parental Mental Illness and Family Wellbeing

Perinatal mental health, trauma-informed relational practice and the early childhood service as a protective environment — grounded in Queensland services and current Australian evidence.

1 in 5Australians experience mental illness annually (AIHW, 2022)
1 in 5new mothers — perinatal anxiety or depression (PANDA, 2023)
1 in 10new fathers — perinatal mental illness (PANDA, 2023)
Be YouFree national initiative — register at beyou.edu.au
Understanding the Context
Bowlby (1988) Attachment TheorySocial Determinants of HealthMasten (2014) ResilienceSAMHSA (2014) Trauma-Informed Practice

Parental mental illness, substance use, perinatal anxiety and depression, grief, disability and family violence are all examples of health and wellbeing contexts affecting families in early childhood settings. The AIHW (2022) reports approximately one in five Australians experiences a mental health condition annually. PANDA (2023) estimates one in five new mothers and one in ten new fathers experience perinatal anxiety or depression. The vast majority of parents who experience mental illness provide loving, safe, committed care for their children — this is the baseline from which all practice in this context begins.

Bowlby's (1988) attachment theory provides foundational grounding: parental mental illness, when severe or unsupported, may affect the consistency and sensitivity of caregiving at critical developmental periods. This is not deterministic — it is mediated by protective factors including treatment access, social support and quality early childhood education (Masten, 2014). Trauma-informed practice (SAMHSA, 2014) prioritises safety, trust, choice, collaboration and empowerment in all interactions.

Impact on Children, Families and Services

Children whose parents experience severe or untreated mental illness may face elevated risk of inconsistent caregiving and disrupted routines. Some children take on caring roles for parents. In services, children may present with anxiety, emotional dysregulation or difficulty forming trusting relationships — communications of unmet need, requiring compassionate interpretation. Services can actively support family mental health through relational quality, emotional literacy embedded throughout the curriculum and outdoor play.

Understanding the Context
Bronfenbrenner (1979) Ecological SystemsCrenshaw (1989) IntersectionalitySAMHSA (2014) Trauma-Informed PracticeMasten (2014) Resilience under chronic stress

Domestic and family violence (DFV) encompasses any pattern of behaviour in an intimate or family relationship used to coerce, control or harm — including physical violence, sexual assault, emotional and psychological abuse, financial abuse and coercive control. Coercive control is not escalating conflict: it is a sustained pattern of surveillance, isolation, humiliation and domination that can be more harmful to children than physical violence alone. The ABS Personal Safety Survey (2023) reports approximately one in six Australian women and one in seventeen men have experienced physical and/or sexual violence from a current or former partner since age 15.

In Queensland, the Domestic and Family Violence Protection Act 2012 (amended 2023) now includes coercive control as a criminal offence. Intersectionality (Crenshaw, 1989) illuminates how overlapping dimensions — Indigenous status, disability, visa status, sexuality and geographic isolation — compound vulnerability and constrain help-seeking.

Two Family Circumstances — Crisis Context
1
Single-Parent Families Escaping DFV
Single parents — most commonly mothers — who have recently escaped a DFV relationship face an acute combination of stressors: emergency or temporary accommodation (caravan parks, refuges, couch-surfing), sudden income loss, emotional and physical trauma, relocation away from their support networks, and often legal proceedings and protection orders running simultaneously. Children in these families experience multiple and rapid changes — new home, new suburb, potentially new service — in a very short period.

These children may arrive at a new early childhood service mid-year, frightened and disoriented, with incomplete records. They may be highly sensitive to loud voices, sudden physical contact or unpredictable routines. The non-offending parent is simultaneously managing crisis, grief and the needs of their children, often with very limited resources.
Service Implication Provide a warm, flexible enrolment process. Contact the receiving service proactively with records (with consent). Establish confidentiality protocols immediately — never share the family's location or contact details under any circumstances.
2
Families Where the Non-Offending Parent Remains
Families where DFV is occurring but the non-offending parent has not yet left face a different and equally complex set of circumstances. Coercive control means the non-offending parent may have severely limited access to money, transport, a phone or decision-making authority. They may be heavily monitored. Children in these families live in environments of fear, unpredictability and secrecy — they may be instructed not to speak about home life and may be deeply protective of their parent.

These families are often not identifiable through enrolment records. Children may not disclose directly but may exhibit persistent hypervigilance, regression, difficulty with transitions or changes in behaviour following weekends or school holidays.
Service Implication Maintain consistent, warm, non-pressuring relationships. Share information about services non-judgementally as general safety information. Document behavioural observations specifically and factually. Report to Queensland Child Safety (1800 177 135) when there are reasonable grounds.
Impact on Children, Families and Services

Children who have witnessed or experienced DFV face elevated population-level risk for developmental, social-emotional and mental health challenges, though individual outcomes depend significantly on protective factors and available support (AIHW, 2022). Chronic stress in environments characterised by fear affects neurological development — particularly the stress-response systems underpinning emotional regulation and executive functioning. In Queensland early childhood settings, children may present with hypervigilance, sleep disturbances, regression or aggressive play — adaptive responses requiring compassionate interpretation.

Social Policy and Australian Responses
National Plan to End Violence Against Women and Children 2022–2032 (DSS, 2022)

Australia's overarching framework for preventing and responding to DFV, structured across prevention, early intervention, response and recovery. Addresses primary prevention through gender inequality.

StrengthPositions DFV as requiring whole-community prevention. Includes children as victim-survivors in their own right.
Critical NoteWaiting times for crisis accommodation in Queensland remain lengthy. Culturally appropriate services for First Nations women and CALD communities remain insufficient.
Queensland DFV Protection Act 2012 (amended 2023)

Queensland's legal framework for DFV protection orders and safety planning. The 2023 amendments introduce coercive control as a criminal offence.

StrengthQueensland among the first Australian jurisdictions to criminalise coercive control.
Critical NoteCriminalising coercive control creates new service system responsibilities. Educators need training to recognise coercive control patterns, not only physical indicators.
Queensland Mandatory Reporting — Child Protection Act 1999

Requires all early childhood educators to report reasonable suspicion of harm or risk of harm — including children's exposure to domestic violence — to Child Safety (1800 177 135) without delay.

StrengthClear, legally enforceable obligation protecting children whose parents may not be able to self-report safely.
Critical NoteMany educators remain uncertain about reporting thresholds for children's exposure to DFV. Regular mandatory reporting training specific to DFV contexts is essential.
Five Evidence-Based Strategies for Practice
01
Trauma-Informed Environmental Design

Maintain calm, consistent, predictable environments. Use low-sensory transitions, consistent educator voices and spaces that do not feel threatening. Interpret hypervigilance as responses to prior threat — not conduct requiring management.

02
Age-Appropriate Body Safety Education

Incorporate body safety as part of everyday curriculum — not a reactive response to identified concerns. Use Australian-developed resources. Teach correct anatomical language, safe and unsafe touch and that worry secrets must be shared with a trusted adult.

03
Non-Judgemental, Confidential Practice

Listen. Affirm experience. Share information about available services without pressure. Never disclose a family's location or contact details to any third party without explicit written consent from the family.

04
Support Continuity of Care During Relocation

Maintain flexible attendance records. When families need to relocate quickly, facilitate rapid documentation transfer and provide a warm written handover to the receiving service.

05
Fulfil Mandatory Reporting Obligations Promptly

Document exact observations, specific behaviours and verbatim disclosures in factual, non-interpretive language. Consult the nominated supervisor. Report to Queensland Child Safety (1800 177 135) when there are reasonable grounds.
